1. HIGH-LEVEL ARCHITECTURE: FROM POLICE FIR TO INTERACTIVE MAP
================================================================================

When a road accident occurs in Gujarat, police officers record an electronic FIR
containing:
  - Geographic Coordinates: Latitude and Longitude (WGS-84 GPS).
  - Severity Level: Fatal, Grievous Injury, Minor Injury (Hospitalized),
    Minor Injury (Non-Hospitalized), or No Injury / Damage Only.
  - Casualties: Count of drivers, passengers, and pedestrians killed or injured.
  - Environmental & Contextual Attributes: Road classification (NH, SH, MDR),
    collision type (head-on, rear-end, hit-pedestrian), light condition, weather,
    and exact timestamp.

The Problem with Raw Point Data:
Plotting 50,000 raw accident pins directly on a map creates an illegible "blob"
of overlapping dots. It fails to answer critical engineering questions:
  - Where are the legal MoRTH blackspots requiring government funding?
  - Which highway stretches need continuous median barriers vs spot junction fixes?
  - Are accidents clustered at a specific intersection or spread across a 2 km curve?

How G-TRISP Solves This:
G-TRISP applies spatial statistics, computational geometry, and civil engineering
standards (MoRTH and IRC:131) to cluster, smooth, rank, and project crash data
into intuitive, color-coded layers.

2. SPATIAL PROJECTIONS: HOW GPS COORDINATES ARE PROJECTED & HANDLED
================================================================================

A fundamental challenge in GIS mapping is that the Earth is an oblate spheroid,
not a flat sheet of graph paper.

2.1 Geographic vs. Projected Coordinate Reference Systems
---------------------------------------------------------
G-TRISP operates across three coordinate systems:

  1. WGS-84 (EPSG:4326) - Input & Storage:
     - Stored as angular coordinates (Latitude, Longitude) in degrees.
     - 1 degree of latitude is always ~110.5 km.
     - But 1 degree of longitude shrinks as you move away from the equator:
       at Gujarat's latitude (~22.5 deg N), 1 degree of longitude is only ~102.8 km.
     - Calculating distances using standard Euclidean math (dx^2 + dy^2) on raw
       degrees causes severe oval distortion.

  2. Local Equirectangular Cartesian Plane - High-Performance Algorithms:
     - For KDE heatmaps, greedy search, and grid clustering, the backend projects
       coordinates onto a localized flat metric plane (x, y) in meters centered at
       the dataset centroid latitude:

       ref_lat_rad = (Average of all latitudes in dataset) * (pi / 180)
       x = lon * 111,320.0 * cos(ref_lat_rad)
       y = lat * 110,540.0

       Where:
         * 111,320.0 meters is the equatorial length of one degree of longitude.
         * cos(ref_lat_rad) scales the longitude accurately for Earth's curvature.
         * 110,540.0 meters is the length of one degree of latitude in Gujarat.

     - Inverse unprojection (converting grid results back to GeoJSON degrees):
       lon = x / (111,320.0 * cos(ref_lat_rad))
       lat = y / 110,540.0

  3. Web Mercator (EPSG:3857) - PostGIS & Frontend MapLibre:
     - Used by the database when computing true geodesic lengths of roads
       (ST_Length in meters).
     - Used by MapLibre GL in the browser for GPU-accelerated map rendering.

3. ACCIDENT ROAD SNAPPING: ELIMINATING GPS DRIFT
================================================================================

Related Files:
  - backend/app/seed/seed_snapped_accidents.py
  - backend/app/utils/coordinate_validator.py
  - frontend/src/components/maps/SnappedAccidentLayers.tsx

3.1 The Practical Problem
-------------------------
Handheld GPS devices and smartphones used by police officers typically have an
accuracy of 10 to 50 meters. When plotted raw, accidents often appear inside
adjacent agricultural fields, private buildings, or in rivers beside bridges.
This makes it impossible to automatically identify which specific road asset,
carriageway, or lane was involved.

3.2 How the Algorithm Works (Spatial Snapping)
---------------------------------------------
1. Spatial Bounding Box Filter:
   For each accident point P(lat, lon), the database expands a small 0.01-degree
   bounding box (~1.1 km) around the point to eliminate 99.9% of unrelated roads.

2. K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) Search:
   Using PostGIS's R-Tree GiST index operator '<->', the database finds the single
   closest physical road line segment with zero table scan overhead:
     ORDER BY road.geometry <-> accident.location LIMIT 1

3. Orthogonal Vector Projection:
   PostGIS drops a mathematical perpendicular line from the raw GPS point onto
   the road line string. The exact intersection is the "Snapped Location":
     snapped_location = ST_ClosestPoint(nearest_road.geometry, accident.location)

4. Drift Distance Calculation:
   The true physical offset (drift error in meters) is computed over the spheroid:
     distance_meters = ST_Distance(accident.location::geography, nearest_road.geometry::geography)

3.3 How It Appears on the Map
-----------------------------
- Hollow / Pale Dot: The original raw police-reported GPS location.
- Solid Colored Dot: The true snapped position resting on the road centerline.
- Connecting Dashed Line: A visual vector linking the original dot to the snapped
  dot, showing the direction and distance of GPS drift.
- Hover / Click Popup: Displays accident ID, date, severity, collision type, road
  classification, and the exact drift distance in meters.

3.4 What It Tells the Engineer
------------------------------
Engineers can immediately verify whether a crash occurred on a multi-lane National
Highway, an adjacent parallel service road, or an exit ramp before heading into
the field for a physical inspection.

4. CRASH DENSITY HEATMAPS (KDE): FINDING MACROSCOPIC HAZARD ZONES
================================================================================

Related Files:
  - backend/app/utils/kde_utils.py
  - backend/app/routes/dashboard_routes/spatial_routes.py
  - frontend/src/components/maps/GeoJsonHeatmapLayers.tsx
  - frontend/src/config/Heapmapconfig.ts

4.1 The Practical Problem
-------------------------
A transport planner reviewing an entire district needs to see the overall
geographical distribution of crash risk without getting bogged down in thousands
of individual markers. They need to identify which corridors have a systematic,
continuous safety deficiency.

4.2 How the Algorithm Works (The Mathematics of KDE)
-----------------------------------------------------
1. Grid Discretization:
   The analysis area is divided into a regular grid of square cells, each
   measuring 25m x 25m (pixel_m = 25).

2. Kernel Smoothing:
   Each crash acts as a smooth "density mound" that radiates influence outward
   up to a search bandwidth R = 500 meters (radius_m = 500).

3. The Quartic (Biweight) Kernel Function:
   For any grid cell center (x, y) at distance d from crash point (px, py):
     u = d / R
     If d <= R:
       K(u) = (3 / pi) * (1 - u^2)^2
     Else:
       K(u) = 0.0

   Why the Quartic Kernel?
   - Compact Support: Influence drops to exactly zero at 500 meters, meaning
     the backend only calculates cells within a local bounding box.
   - Continuous Derivative: The slope gently reaches zero at 500 meters, ensuring
     there are no artificial circular steps or sharp rings in the heat visualization.

4. Weighted Severity Density:
   To ensure fatal crashes stand out over property damage, each crash point is
   multiplied by its policy severity weight w[i]:
     Total Density D(x, y) = Sum of all i [ w[i] * K(d[i] / R) ]

   Severity Weights:
     - Fatal:                      10.0
     - Grievous Injury:             5.0
     - Minor Hospitalized:          3.0
     - Minor Non-Hospitalized:      2.0
     - Non-Injury / Damage Only:    1.0
     (In the unweighted mode, w[i] = 1.0 for all crashes)

5. Peak Normalization:
   Raw densities vary wildly based on sample size. The grid is normalized to a
   universal 0 to 100 percentage scale:
     Normalized Density = (D(x, y) / Maximum Grid Density) * 100.0

6. Dynamic Downsampling:
   To guarantee high-speed rendering in the browser, if positive cells exceed
   60,000, a dynamic stride step is calculated:
     stride = max(1, ceil( sqrt(positive_cells / 60000) ))
   The grid is sliced: density_grid[::stride, ::stride].

4.3 How It Appears on the Map
-----------------------------
Rendered as a continuous, glowing color surface:
  - Transparent / Pale Blue: Baseline density (isolated, infrequent incidents).
  - Green / Yellow: Moderate density (emerging crash concentration).
  - Bright Red / Deep Burgundy: Peak density hotspot (top 5% crash intensity).
  - Dynamic Zoom Behavior: As the engineer zooms in from state overview (zoom 7)
    to street level (zoom 14), MapLibre's WebGL shader dynamically tightens the
    radius (from 34px down to 12px) to reveal the exact hazardous road segment.

5. MoRTH BLACKSPOT DETECTION (500-METER SECTION ANALYSIS)
================================================================================

Related Files:
  - backend/app/utils/blackspot_utils.py
  - backend/app/core/gis_config.py
  - frontend/src/components/maps/DbscanBlackspotDetectionLayers.tsx
  - frontend/src/config/blackspotConfig.ts

5.1 The Practical Problem & Ministry Criteria
---------------------------------------------
Road safety funding in India is formally governed by the Ministry of Road
Transport and Highways (MoRTH) blackspot protocol. An official blackspot is defined as:
  "A road section of about 500 meters in length in which either 5 or more
   fatal/grievous injury accidents occurred during the last 3 calendar years,
   OR where 10 or more fatalities occurred during the same period."

5.2 How the Algorithm Works (Greedy Density-First Sweep)
-------------------------------------------------------
1. Radial Proxy:
   A 250-meter circular radius around crashes represents a 500-meter linear
   road diameter. Distances are calculated using the spherical Haversine formula:
     dlat = (lat2 - lat1) * (pi / 180)
     dlon = (lon2 - lon1) * (pi / 180)
     a = sin(dlat/2)^2 + cos(lat1*pi/180) * cos(lat2*pi/180) * sin(dlon/2)^2
     distance = 2 * 6,371,000 * asin(sqrt(a))

2. Spatial Hash Grid (O(n) Optimization):
   Crashes are indexed into 250m grid cells. Distance checks only query the point's
   own cell and 8 adjacent neighbors, eliminating sluggish O(n^2) loops.

3. Density-First Selection:
   The algorithm tallies crashes within 250m of every point and places them in
   an O(1) Bucket Queue. It repeatedly picks the densest remaining crash as the
   anchor point.

4. Qualification Evaluation:
   All crashes within 250m of the anchor are tested against MoRTH rules:
     Qualifying Crashes = Count of (Fatal + Grievous Injury crashes)
     Total Fatalities   = Sum of deaths across all member crashes
     Passes if: (Qualifying Crashes >= 5) OR (Total Fatalities >= 10)

5. Cluster Extraction & Neighbor Subtraction:
   If qualified:
     - A permanent Blackspot entity is created.
     - All constituent crashes are assigned to this blackspot and removed from
       the available pool.
     - Surrounding neighbor counts are updated so no crash is counted in two
       different blackspots.
   If not qualified:
     - The anchor point is removed from consideration.

5.3 Priority Scoring (Severity Ranking Formula)
-----------------------------------------------
To rank blackspots so funding goes to the deadliest locations first:
  Priority Score = (Fatal Crashes * 10)
                 + (Grievous Injury Crashes * 5)
                 + (Minor Hospitalized Crashes * 3)
                 + (Minor Non-Hospitalized Crashes * 2)
                 + (No Injury / Property Damage Crashes * 1)

5.4 Dynamic Equal-Interval Risk Tiers
--------------------------------------
The score distribution of blackspots in the current filter selection is divided
into 5 equal intervals:
  Interval = (Max Score - Min Score) / 5.0

  Score Range                              Risk Tier              Map Color
  -------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Min Score to (Min + Interval)            Low Risk               #FD8D3C (Orange)
  (Min + Interval) to (Min + 2*Interval)   Medium Risk            #FC4E2A (Orange-Red)
  (Min + 2*Interval) to (Min + 3*Interval) High Risk              #E31A1C (Red)
  (Min + 3*Interval) to (Min + 4*Interval) Very High Risk         #BD0026 (Dark Red)
  (Min + 4*Interval) to Max Score          Critical Blackspot     #800026 (Burgundy)

5.5 How It Appears on the Map
-----------------------------
- Circular Area (500m diameter): Transparent halo showing the spatial footprint.
- Centroid Pin: A prominent center marker sized proportionally to the crash count:
    Radius = sqrt(crash_count) * zoom_scale
- Marker Color: Matches the Priority Tier (Orange up to Burgundy).
- Interaction: Clicking any pin opens a full inspection dossier with total
  killed, grievous injuries, vehicle breakdown (trucks, 2-wheelers, cars),
  and a button to generate an official Blackspot PDF Report.

6. DBSCAN BLACKSPOT DETECTION (ISOLATING INDEPENDENT HAZARDS)
================================================================================

Related File: backend/app/utils/blackspot_utils.py (dbscan_blackspots)

6.1 The Practical Problem
-------------------------
In heavily congested urban corridors (e.g., Surat Ring Road or SG Highway in
Ahmedabad), accidents occur continuously over 4 to 5 kilometers. Standard density
clustering algorithms tend to merge the entire 5 km highway into one enormous,
unmanageable cluster. But civil engineering tenders and municipal budgets are
awarded for specific 500-meter remedial works (such as rebuilding an intersection
or constructing an underpass).

6.2 How the Algorithm Works (Non-Maximum Overlap Suppression)
------------------------------------------------------------
1. Fixed Neighborhood Search:
   Using radius epsilon = 250m and MinPoints = 5, every crash with >= 5 neighbors
   is flagged as a candidate cluster center.

2. Descending Sort:
   Candidate centers are sorted strictly by crash density (densest centers first).

3. Overlap Suppression Rule:
   The algorithm selects the densest candidate center first. Then, it evaluates
   every subsequent candidate center and enforces a minimum separation distance:
     Euclidean_Distance(candidate_center, accepted_center) > 500 meters (2 * epsilon)

   Any candidate center within 500 meters of an already accepted center is suppressed.

4. Result:
   Every identified blackspot is guaranteed to be a distinct, non-overlapping
   500-meter road segment that can be independently inspected, audited, and funded.

7. IRC:131 BLACKSPOT CATEGORIZATION (AATC RATE BENCHMARK METHOD)
================================================================================

Related File: backend/app/utils/irc_blackspot_utils.py

7.1 The Practical Problem
-------------------------
A simple crash count (e.g. ">= 5 crashes") creates geographical bias: busy urban
avenues with 50,000 vehicles/day easily accumulate 5 minor crashes, while a
deadly rural highway with low traffic volume might have 4 high-speed fatal crashes
and be completely ignored.

To fix this, the Indian Roads Congress code IRC:131-2022 ("Guidelines for
Identification and Treatment of Blackspots") uses a statistical Rate-Quality
Control method that benchmarks localized crash frequency against the overall
road network's historical average.

7.2 Mathematical Formulations
-----------------------------
Step 1: Calculate Average Annual Total Crashes (AATC)
  For a candidate 500m section containing N crashes over Y years (default Y = 3.0):
    AATC = N / Y

Step 2: Calculate Expected Network Benchmark Crash Rate (M)
  What is the expected average crash count for any arbitrary 500-meter road section
  in the district during one year?
    M = Total_District_Crashes / Total_Road_Length_km / 2.0 / Years_of_Data
  (We divide by 2.0 because 1 kilometer contains two 500-meter sections).

Step 3: Assign IRC Category by Multipliers of M
  A road section is categorized based on how many times worse its crash rate is
  compared to the network average M:

  Observed Crash Rate (AATC)     IRC Category   Risk Classification   Color
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
  AATC >= 15 * M                 Category 1     Critical / Highest    #D73027 (Red)
  10 * M <= AATC < 15 * M        Category 2     High Priority         #FD8D3C (Orange)
  5 * M  <= AATC < 10 * M        Category 3     Medium Priority       #FFEB64 (Yellow)
  3 * M  <= AATC < 5 * M         Category 4     Low Priority          #91CF60 (Green)
  AATC < 3 * M                   Unclassified   Normal / Baseline     (Not flagged)

7.3 Why This Matters for Policy Makers
--------------------------------------
Under IRC:131, a rural highway spot with 5 crashes can be flagged as Category 1
(Critical) if the rural district's average M is very low, ensuring high-speed rural
fatality zones receive equal priority to urban intersections.

8. NETWORK-CONSTRAINED BLACKSPOTS (TRUE LINEAR HIGHWAY CHAINAGE)
================================================================================

Related File: backend/app/utils/network_blackspot_utils.py

8.1 The Practical Problem
-------------------------
Circular 250m radii assume the terrain is flat. At multi-level flyovers, trumpet
interchanges, or parallel service roads, a 250m circle captures crashes from the
elevated expressway, the ground roundabout, and the local service lane simultaneously,
mixing up completely separate traffic streams.

8.2 How the Algorithm Works (Linear Referencing System - LRS)
------------------------------------------------------------
1. Road Linear Fraction:
   Using PostGIS ST_LineLocatePoint, each accident is mapped to its exact fraction
   f (from 0.0 to 1.0) along the digitized road centerline:
     Distance along road (meters) = fraction * Total_Road_Length_meters

2. 1D Continuous Sliding Window:
   Along each road, accidents are sorted by linear chainage (Ch 0+000 to Ch End).
   A window of length 500 meters slides along the centerline in 50-meter steps:
     Window = [start_m, min(start_m + 500m, road_length)]

3. Cluster Centering:
   To prevent empty edge padding, the window shifts to center itself over the
   exact cluster of accidents:
     center_m = (min_accident_dist + max_accident_dist) / 2.0
     best_start_m = max(0.0, center_m - 250m)
     best_end_m   = min(road_length, center_m + 250m)

4. Non-Maximum Overlap Suppression on the Line:
   Windows are ranked by MoRTH priority score. Segments overlapping any higher-ranked
   segment on the same road are suppressed.

8.3 What It Tells the Engineer
------------------------------
This produces exact highway chainage milestones (e.g., "NH-48, KM 142.250 to KM 142.750"),
allowing National Highway Authority of India (NHAI) engineers to inspect the exact
physical asset.

9. CONTINUOUS RISK CORRIDORS (MERGING HAZARDOUS HIGHWAY STRETCHES)
================================================================================

Related Files:
  - backend/app/utils/corridor_utils.py
  - backend/app/routes/dashboard_routes/corridors_routes.py
  - frontend/src/components/maps/RiskCorridorLayers.tsx
  - frontend/src/config/riskCorridorConfig.ts

9.1 The Practical Problem
-------------------------
If a highway has three consecutive 500m blackspots separated by only 50 meters,
treating them as isolated spots is an engineering mistake. The root cause is
systemic along the corridor: an undivided 4-lane cross-section, lack of street
lighting, absence of pedestrian overpasses, or uncontrolled commercial ribbon
development over a 2 km stretch.

9.2 How Risk Corridors Are Generated (Topological Merging)
---------------------------------------------------------
1. Order Blackspot Segments:
   All identified 500m blackspot segments on a given road are sorted by start distance.

2. Gap Distance Evaluation:
   The gap between consecutive segments is evaluated:
     Gap = Next_Segment_Start - Current_Corridor_End

3. Merging Condition:
   If Gap <= 100 meters:
     - The two segments are merged into one continuous corridor.
     - New Corridor End = max(Current_End, Next_End)
     - Accident IDs are combined using mathematical set union:
         Corridor_Accidents = Set(Accidents_A) UNION Set(Accidents_B)
       (Accidents in the overlap zone are counted only once).

4. Corridor Accident Density:
   For a merged corridor of length L_km:
     Accident Density = Total Unique Accidents / L_km  (accidents per km)

5. PostGIS Line Slicing:
   The exact polyline geometry is sliced from the road network table:
     ST_LineSubstring(road.geometry, start_fraction, end_fraction)

9.3 How the Corridor Score Is Calculated
------------------------------------------
For every continuous corridor, its risk score is computed using the MoRTH severity weights:
  Weighted Score = (Fatal_Crashes * 10)
                 + (Grievous_Injury_Crashes * 5)
                 + (Minor_Hospitalized_Crashes * 3)
                 + (Minor_Non_Hospitalized_Crashes * 2)

  Priority Score = Weighted Score

In addition, the physical crash density is measured:
  Corridor Length (km) = (end_m - start_m) / 1000.0
  Accident Density     = Total_Unique_Accidents / Corridor_Length_km

Corridors are sorted deterministically:
  1st: Priority Score (descending)
  2nd: Accident Density (descending)
  3rd: Corridor ID (for stable ranking)

9.4 How Priority Levels Are Assigned (Dynamic Equal-Interval Binning)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Priority tiers are NOT assigned using fixed arbitrary numbers (like 250 or 150).
Instead, the backend dynamically calculates 5 equal intervals based on the actual
distribution of scores among all corridors returned for the active filter query:

  min_score = Lowest priority score among all detected corridors
  max_score = Highest priority score among all detected corridors
  interval  = (max_score - min_score) / 5.0

  Thresholds:
    Low Cutoff       = min_score + (1 * interval)
    Medium Cutoff    = min_score + (2 * interval)
    High Cutoff      = min_score + (3 * interval)
    Very High Cutoff = min_score + (4 * interval)

  Priority Tier Classification:
    Tier         Score Range                     Map Color
    ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
    Low          Score < Low Cutoff              #00E676 (Vivid Emerald Green)
    Medium       Low Cutoff <= Score < Med       #0099FF (Vivid Dodger Blue)
    High         Med Cutoff <= Score < High      #A855F7 (Vivid Electric Purple)
    Very High    High Cutoff <= Score < V.High   #FF6A00 (Vivid Safety Orange)
    Critical     Score >= V.High Cutoff          #FF0033 (Electric Crimson Red)

  (Special Case: If all corridors have the exact same score, all are set to "Medium").

10. STATEWIDE DISTRICT CHOROPLETH MAP (ADMINISTRATIVE RISK BALANCING)
================================================================================

Related Files:
  - frontend/src/components/maps/GujaratChoroplethMap.tsx
  - backend/app/routes/geo.py
  - backend/app/routes/dashboard_routes/analytics_routes.py

10.1 The Practical Problem
--------------------------
For state-level officials (such as the Director General of Police or the Road
Safety Commissioner), the overview map must clearly communicate how the 33
districts of Gujarat compare against each other.

10.2 Square-Root Perceptual Color Scaling
-----------------------------------------
In Gujarat, accident counts are heavily skewed: mega-districts like Ahmedabad
or Surat record 2,500+ crashes annually, whereas rural districts like Dang or
Tapi record around 150.

If a standard linear color scale is used:
  - Ahmedabad and Surat become dark navy blue.
  - The remaining 31 districts all collapse into the lowest, palest ice-blue band,
    completely hiding meaningful differences between mid-sized districts.

G-TRISP solves this using a Square Root Scale:
  Scale Value t = sqrt( District_Accidents / Max_District_Accidents )
  Fill Color = InterpolateRGB("#EAF0FE" [light blue], "#1E3A8A" [dark navy])(t)

Mathematical Example:
  - Ahmedabad (2,500 crashes): sqrt(2500 / 2500) = 1.00 -> Darkest Navy Blue
  - Rajkot (900 crashes):      sqrt(900 / 2500)  = 0.60 -> Strong Medium Blue
  - Anand (400 crashes):       sqrt(400 / 2500)  = 0.40 -> Visible Cyan-Blue
  - Dang (100 crashes):        sqrt(100 / 2500)  = 0.20 -> Pale Ice Blue

Result: Differences between developing districts remain sharp and distinguishable,
ensuring balanced state-level budget allocations.

10.3 Polygon Ring Winding Correction (Shoelace Formula)
-------------------------------------------------------
D3's spherical map engine requires outer boundary rings to wind clockwise
(Signed Area < 0). If the database supplies counter-clockwise winding, D3 draws
the complement of the polygon (filling the entire world except Gujarat).
The algorithm calculates polygon area using the Shoelace formula (Green's theorem):
  Area = 0.5 * Sum of all i [ (x[i] * y[i+1]) - (x[i+1] * y[i]) ]
If Area > 0, the coordinate sequence is reversed before path rendering.

  - backend/app/routes/dashboard_routes/analytics_routes.py

11.1 Operational Diurnal Buckets
--------------------------------
Crash timestamps are grouped into 4 functional traffic shifts:
  - Morning (05:00 to 11:59):   Morning commute rush, school transit.
  - Afternoon (12:00 to 16:59): Commercial freight, inter-city bus movement.
  - Evening (17:00 to 20:59):   Evening rush hour, dusk lighting transition.
  - Night (21:00 to 04:59):     Long-haul freight, driver fatigue, alcohol risk.

11.2 The 24 x 7 Hour-Day Interaction Matrix
--------------------------------------------
A 2D contingency matrix of 24 rows (hours 0 to 23) by 7 columns (Monday through
Sunday) tallies every crash:
  Matrix[h, d] = Count of accidents where hour == h AND day == d

The dashboard calculates the peak operational risk moment via argmax:
  (Peak_Hour, Peak_Day) = Indices of maximum count in Matrix

This immediately tells traffic police leadership when to deploy highway patrol
interceptors and sobriety checkpoints.
